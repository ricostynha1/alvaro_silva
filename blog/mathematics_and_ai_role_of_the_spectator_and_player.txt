These past days have been a mix of excitement and grief. All induced by the AI-heavily assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. When I tried to explain to family and friends, they could not understand why solving a problem they had never heard of was affecting me so much, so I feel the need to write an exposition for a general audience. I will use basketball as a metaphor.

I always enjoyed math from an early age. I enjoyed solving the mathematical puzzles I encountered as a child and as a teenager, and I also participated in some math competitions. That is something I always liked. When it was time to choose a major, I opted not to pursue mathematics but to study electrical and computer engineering. Nevertheless, the fascination remained, and I continued to pay very close attention to advances in mathematics.

So I relate, in the metaphor, to the role of a spectator fan in mathematics and its advancements. And I was keen to see the mathematicians ("that on the metaphor are my home team players") reaching higher and higher stances of knowledge even though I could not understand the proofs they produced, the "plays" they made.

Some examples from these years include: 2013, Yitang Zhang's breakthrough on the Twin Prime conjecture. That was a remarkable play that proved there are infinitely many pairs of prime numbers separated by less than 70 million. And then it was successfully improved by a joint team effort by the Polymath project, which managed to reduce the gap to 246; it was a marvelous collaboration from the "team" that felt to a spectator like myself like a great moment, a magic moment.

Further, some years ago, I remember hearing about Terence Tao's improvements to the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, in which he found, in a simplified setting, that finite-time blow-up could occur. That was the first time I encountered the Millennium Prize Problems in mathematics ("the highest accomplishments in this game").

And as a spectator who happens to learn about a new prize in this sport, I then started searching the others; P=NP is very close to home, as I did basically electrical and computer engineering and now am pursuing a PhD in formal methods, the Navier-Stokes problems; it is simple enough to explain for a spectator to have a good picture of the problem, and finally the Riemann Hypothesis. Although it is not easy to understand, it requires some digging. Once the spectator finds the Euler Product formula that relates the Riemann Zeta function to the product over prime numbers, the spectator can start to feel awe at the connection between that function and prime numbers.

Still, I am merely a spectator. I did try to read Tao's paper on the Navier–Stokes problem, and I could not get past the second page. There is simply too much baggage in mathematical papers written only to be understandable to other players, making them too overwhelming for a spectator. Yet I could, all the same, experience the awe of the discovery and the buildup of knowledge.

Fast-forward to now: the Navier–Stokes and the Riemann Hypothesis have been solved by AI systems (with an unclear amount of human expertise involved, millions of dollars of compute, and proprietary LLM models that are not available to the public).

This changes the game. These were not human teams of mathematical players. In the analogy, it's like the team being replaced by one human and four robots, where whenever a robot got better, the results within reach became easier to achieve. Whenever the robot was more expensive, the problems tackled could be harder. And here we are at the moment: as a spectator, I still enjoy seeing records broken and feel excited watching knowledge advance (with the Riemann Hypothesis solved, a huge corpus of results that previously depended on the Riemann Hypothesis being true are now theorems). Yet I am grieving for my team. The team that AI is replacing. The stories of mathematicians who, through sheer intellect and perseverance, accomplished those goals (like Yitang Zhang, Andrew Wiles, and others) may be gone, and for that I am grieving. Also, the game seems to have been replaced with a pay-to-win game, which is always a meta that gamers and spectators usually do not enjoy.

Even Levent Alpöge, the Anthropic mathematician who was involved in solving RH, said in his 4-page communique that he did not understand the proof (converted into pages, it would be 250,000 pages). But because the proof is formally verified in Lean with 17 million lines of code, it is correct. Note that we can only know this proof is correct because, in this new game, computer scientists and engineers like Leonardo de Moura created software that ensures the robots' plays (which are not human-like) are valid and actually achieve the goal. Therefore, even more than AI itself, these proof checkers are the missing piece that allows these systems to be so good at math. And not, for now, as much at other games.

So the human team is being benched into a spectator role, just like I already am. We will be watching the robots play and enjoying the results more or less without full understanding, while losing the dream of becoming the next human player altogether in a pay-to-win game. We may be witnessing a paradigm shift in mathematics, which makes me uneasy. I am not a player in mathematics, but we are all players in some game, and we are seeing the results of the first robot substitution taking place in another arena. We can see the writing on the wall: it will reach my game too, and we will all become spectators.

I hope the game is made for spectators to enjoy and is constructed in a way that lets them push their own understanding, even without playing. For all the upcoming games impacted, let's push as far as we can in our own games while we're still playing, and let's pressure the game's institutions to align the game and the teams building the robots with the spectators' goals.
